# Kitchen science

Mechanisms and failure modes for the kitchen simulation. Sections: Heat transfer · Browning · Proteins and meat · Salt, brines and marinades · Eggs · Starches, grains and legumes · Vegetables · Emulsions and sauces · Dairy · Gels and thickeners · Fats and frying · Enzymes · Fermentation and pickling · Water, reduction and alcohol · Food safety · Altitude.

## Heat transfer

- **Thickness rules time.** Heat diffuses, so cooking time scales roughly with thickness squared: double the thickness, about four times the time. A 2.5 cm (1 inch) steak and a 5 cm roast are different problems.
- **Water caps temperature at 100°C (212°F)** at sea level, lower at altitude (about 1°C less per 300 m). Wet food can't brown until its surface dries. A pressure cooker reaches about 120°C, which is why it is fast.
- **Crowding steams.** Food releases water; if it can't evaporate faster than it appears, the pan drops to ~100°C and food boils in its juices. Leave space (pieces not touching), cook in batches, preheat the pan, and don't stir browning food.
- **Pans:** cast iron and carbon steel store heat (searing, cornbread, crust); stainless is responsive and builds fond for pan sauces; nonstick is for eggs and delicate fish at medium heat (avoid empty high-heat preheating). A sheet pan's dark surface browns more than a light one; glass and ceramic heat slowly and hold heat.
- **Ovens** run hot or cold by 10-25°C and have hot spots. Convection (fan) cooks about 25% faster: drop 15°C (25°F) or check early. The broiler is radiant heat from above, like an upside-down grill. Air fryers are small convection ovens.
- **Carryover cooking:** interior temperature keeps rising after leaving the heat, about 1-3°C for steaks and chops and 3-8°C for large roasts. Pull early.
- **Resting** lets temperature equalize so the center finishes and less juice runs when cut. Rest steaks 5 minutes, roasts 15-30. The "juices redistribute" explanation is overstated; the main effects are carryover and slightly thickened juices.

## Browning

- **Maillard reaction** (amino acids + reducing sugars) produces roasted, meaty, toasty flavors and brown color. It is slow below ~120°C and fast around 140-165°C (285-330°F). It needs a dry surface. It speeds up with higher pH: a pinch of baking soda browns onions faster (and softens them); baking powder plus salt on chicken skin, dried uncovered in the fridge, gives shatteringly crisp skin. Sugars accelerate it (milk, honey, soy glazes brown fast and burn fast).
- **Caramelization** (sugar alone) starts around 110°C for fructose and 160°C for sucrose, giving nutty, butterscotch and bitter notes as it deepens.
- **"Caramelized onions"** are mostly Maillard plus caramelization over medium-low heat for 40-60 minutes. A splash of water to deglaze the fond along the way speeds things up without burning. Recipes promising them in 10 minutes are wrong.
- **Fond** (browned bits stuck to the pan) is concentrated flavor: deglaze with wine, stock, vinegar or water and scrape.
- **Char and pyrolysis** above ~200°C: a little gives smoky bitterness (charred peppers, blackened scallions, wok hei); a lot tastes acrid.
- **Toasting** nuts, seeds, spices, flour (for roux), rice (for pilaf and risotto) and breadcrumbs builds Maillard flavor cheaply. Nuts: 175°C (350°F) for 6-10 minutes, until fragrant and a shade darker; they burn quickly after that.

## Proteins and meat

Muscle proteins change at predictable temperatures:
- ~50°C: myosin sets, meat firms and turns opaque (rare to medium-rare).
- ~60°C: collagen sheath shrinks and squeezes out juice (medium).
- ~66-70°C: actin sets, meat turns dry and tight (well done).
- Collagen converts to gelatin slowly from ~70°C, fast near 90°C over hours.

So: **lean, tender cuts** (tenderloin, strip, chicken breast, pork loin, fish) are cooked hot and fast to a precise temperature. **Collagen-rich cuts** (chuck, short rib, brisket, pork shoulder, lamb shoulder, shanks, chicken thighs) need long cooking at 80-95°C internal until fork-tender; they are moist because gelatin coats the fibers, not because the fibers hold water. Braise at a bare simmer or in a 150-165°C (300-325°F) oven; a hard boil makes stringy, dry meat. Chicken thighs are best at 79-90°C (175-195°F) internal, beyond what is "safe", because their connective tissue softens.

- **Safety can be a time-temperature combination**: chicken held at 66°C (150°F) for about 3 minutes is as safe as reaching 74°C (165°F) instantly (USDA time-temperature lethality data). This is how juicy breast meat stays safe.
- **Reverse sear** (low oven to 8-10°C below target, then a very hot pan) gives even edge-to-edge doneness for thick steaks and roasts. Thin steaks: just sear hard.
- **Slice against the grain** for flank, skirt, brisket and hanger; it shortens the fibers.
- **Ground meat:** keep it cold and handle it little. Salt mixed into ground meat dissolves myosin and makes it springy and sausage-like (great for meatballs and kebabs, wrong for burgers). Salt burgers only on the outside just before cooking.
- **Velveting** (Chinese technique): toss thinly sliced meat with a little cornstarch, egg white or water, and soy; or 1/4 tsp baking soda per 250 g for 15-20 minutes, then rinse. Higher pH and starch keep stir-fried meat silky.
- **Poultry skin** crisps when dry: pat dry, salt ahead and leave uncovered in the fridge overnight; start skin-side down in a cold or medium pan to render fat.
- **Fish:** delicate collagen sets at low temperatures. Most fish is moist at 50-55°C (120-130°F); salmon is silky at 46-50°C (115-122°F). FDA guidance is 63°C (145°F). Dry the skin well, start in a hot oiled pan, press for 10 seconds so it doesn't curl, and cook mostly skin-side down. Pull when it flakes under gentle pressure.
- **Shrimp** cook in 2-3 minutes; an opaque "C" shape is done, a tight "O" is overdone. A brief brine (salt plus a pinch of baking soda, 15 minutes) makes them snappy.

## Salt, brines and marinades

- **Dry brining:** 1-1.5% salt by meat weight, from 45 minutes up to 2 days ahead, uncovered in the fridge for poultry skin. Salt draws out moisture, dissolves into it, then is reabsorbed, seasoning deeper and helping the meat hold water. For steaks, salt either right before cooking or at least 40 minutes ahead; in between the surface is wet with brine and won't sear well.
- **Wet brining:** 5-6% salt solution (50-60 g per liter) for 1-4 hours for poultry and pork. Juicy but can turn spongy and dilute flavor; dry brining is usually better at home. Equilibrium brine (1-2% of the total weight of water plus meat) seasons exactly and can't over-salt.
- **Marinades** penetrate only a few millimeters. Salt (soy, fish sauce, salt) is the part that works deeply. Acid more than a couple of hours makes the surface mushy and chalky (seafood within 15-30 minutes). Yogurt and buttermilk marinades are gentle and tenderize mildly. Sugar and oil help browning. Pat the surface dry before searing. A marinade that touched raw meat must be boiled before it's served as a sauce.
- **Salting vegetables** (cucumber, eggplant, cabbage, tomato, zucchini) at 1-2% by weight for 15-30 minutes draws water out, concentrates flavor and prevents watery dishes. Drain and taste before adding more salt.
- **Bread and doughs:** salt tightens gluten and slows yeast; add it with the flour, not directly onto yeast for long contact.

## Eggs

- Whites begin to set around 62°C and are firm by ~80°C; yolks thicken from ~65°C and set by ~70°C. Low, slow heat gives creamy eggs; high heat gives rubber.
- Scrambled: low heat, constant movement, pull while still slightly wet (carryover). Salt beaten in 15 minutes ahead gives more tender curds.
- Boiled (large, from the fridge, into boiling water): 6 minutes for jammy, 7 for soft-set, 10-11 for hard. Ice bath immediately. Steaming peels better than boiling. Older eggs peel easier.
- Custards set around 80°C. Stirred custard (crème anglaise) is done at 77-82°C (170-180°F) and curdles past ~85°C. Baked custards: pull when the center wobbles slightly. Cheesecake: pull at 65-68°C (150-155°F) in the center.
- Egg foams: a clean, fat-free bowl; add sugar after soft peaks form; a little acid (cream of tartar or lemon) stabilizes. Room-temperature whites whip to more volume.

## Starches, grains and legumes

- **Gelatinization:** starch granules swell and thicken from about 55-70°C (it varies by starch). Cornstarch reaches full thickening near boiling and must simmer about a minute (egg yolk enzymes thin unboiled cornstarch custards). Flour must cook several minutes to lose its raw taste. Arrowroot and tapioca thicken at lower temperatures, stay clear, but thin with long cooking. Potato starch is strongest but breaks down with prolonged stirring.
- **Roux** thickening power drops as it darkens: a dark roux thickens about a third as much as a blond one.
- **Retrogradation:** cooked starch firms as it cools (stale bread, hard leftover rice). Day-old, fridge-dried rice makes better fried rice. Cooled starches have more resistant starch.
- **Pasta:** salted water at about 1% (10 g per liter) seasons the pasta. Starchy cooking water is an emulsifier: finish pasta in the sauce for 1-2 minutes with a splash of it, then fat (butter, olive oil, cheese) off the heat for a glossy sauce that clings. Cook 1-2 minutes short of package time when finishing in sauce. Don't rinse unless it's for a cold salad or Asian noodles.
- **Rice:** rinse long-grain and short-grain rice until the water runs mostly clear for separate grains (not for risotto). See `numbers.md` for water ratios. Rest covered 10 minutes off the heat after cooking. Risotto: toast arborio in fat, add hot stock gradually, stir often; it is done al dente and flowing (all'onda), not stiff.
- **Potatoes:** starchy russets for fluffy mash, baking and fries; waxy reds and fingerlings hold shape for salads and roasting; Yukon Golds sit between. Start in cold salted water for even cooking. Mash gently: food processors and blenders burst cells and make glue. For crisp roast potatoes, parboil in water with 1/2 tsp baking soda per 2 liters, rough up the surfaces, then roast hot in plenty of fat. Fries: soak or rinse cut potatoes, then fry twice (about 150°C to cook through, about 190°C to crisp).
- **Legumes:** soak dried beans in salted water (about 1% salt) for creamier interiors and intact skins; salt doesn't toughen beans, that's a myth. Acid, sugar and hard water (calcium) keep beans firm, so add tomatoes and vinegar once beans are already tender. A pinch of baking soda speeds softening (chickpeas for hummus). Old beans can take hours. Lentils need no soaking: red lentils collapse in 15-20 minutes, brown and green hold shape in 20-30, French green (du Puy) stay firmest.
- **Red kidney beans** contain phytohaemagglutinin: boil hard for at least 10 minutes. Slow cookers on low don't get hot enough. Canned beans are already safe.

## Vegetables

- **Pectin** holds plant cells together. It softens with heat and alkalinity and stays firm with acid and calcium. Vegetables cooked in acidic sauces (tomato, wine) stay firm longer; add potatoes or carrots before the acid, or parcook them.
- **Green vegetables:** chlorophyll turns olive-drab with acid and long cooking. Cook greens fast (blanch in heavily salted boiling water for 1-3 minutes, shock in ice water if serving cold) and add acid at the table, not in advance.
- **Roasting** (200-230°C, 400-450°F), spread out with oil and salt, is the easiest flavor upgrade for most vegetables: browning plus concentration. Cut pieces to the same size; put flat sides down on the pan.
- **Mushrooms** release water first, then brown. Cook them in a hot pan without crowding, and salt after they brown. Water-sautéing then browning works well too.
- **Sweating vs. sautéing:** sweating (medium-low, a pinch of salt, maybe a lid) softens alliums without color for a sweet base; sautéing (medium-high) browns them for savory depth.
- **Tomatoes:** the gel around the seeds is the most savory part, so keep it. Salt raw tomatoes 10 minutes before serving. Out of season, canned whole peeled tomatoes beat pale fresh ones.

## Emulsions and sauces

- **Emulsifiers:** lecithin (egg yolk), mustard, honey (viscosity), garlic, starch (pasta water, flour), proteins (casein in cheese, gelatin), and finely blended solids.
- **Vinaigrette:** 3 parts oil to 1 acid is classic; 2:1 is punchier. Dissolve salt in the acid first. Mustard holds it together longer.
- **Mayonnaise and aioli:** one yolk can emulsify well over a cup of oil. Add the first oil drop by drop until it thickens, then stream. Broken? Start over with a fresh yolk (or a teaspoon of water) and whisk the broken mixture in slowly.
- **Butter sauces** (beurre blanc, pan sauces mounted with butter) hold at about 50-60°C. Above that, the butterfat separates. Whisk in cold butter off the heat; if it breaks, whisk in a splash of cold cream or water.
- **Pan sauce:** remove the meat, pour off excess fat, sweat shallots, deglaze (wine, stock or vinegar), reduce until syrupy, take off the heat, swirl in cold butter, adjust salt and acid, and add resting juices.
- **Cheese sauces:** use low heat; finely grated cheese added off the heat. Aged cheeses (parmesan, pecorino, aged cheddar) break and clump with too much heat. Make cacio e pepe and other aged-cheese sauces by whisking cheese into lukewarm starchy water to a paste, then tossing with pasta off the heat. **Sodium citrate** (about 2-3% of the cheese's weight) makes any cheese melt smooth like processed cheese. A little cornstarch tossed with shredded cheese also helps.
- **Gravy:** fat plus flour (roux) plus stock. About 2 tbsp each fat and flour per cup of liquid makes a medium gravy. Whisk; strain if lumpy.

## Dairy

- Higher-fat dairy resists curdling. Heavy cream (36% or more fat) can boil with acid and salt; milk, half-and-half, yogurt and sour cream curdle when boiled, especially with acid. Temper yogurt by whisking in some hot liquid first, add it off the heat, or stabilize it with a teaspoon of flour or cornstarch per cup.
- Whipping needs 30% fat or more and cold cream, bowl and whisk. Stop at soft peaks for folding and topping; overwhipped cream turns grainy, then into butter (rescue: fold in a splash of liquid cream).
- Brown butter: melt over medium heat, swirling. It foams (water boiling off), the foam settles, the milk solids turn hazelnut-brown and smell nutty. Pour it out of the pan immediately. Butter loses about 15-20% of its weight as water.
- Fresh cheese: heat milk to about 85-90°C and add acid (lemon juice or vinegar) to make ricotta-style curds or paneer.

## Gels and thickeners

- **Gelatin:** bloom in cold liquid 5 minutes, dissolve in warm (not boiling) liquid. One packet (7 g, 2¼ tsp) sets about 2 cups (480 ml) firmly; use about 1 packet per 2½-3 cups for a soft panna cotta. Raw pineapple, kiwi, papaya, mango, fig and ginger contain proteases that prevent setting (canned or cooked fruit is fine).
- **Agar** (vegetarian) must boil 1-2 minutes, sets around 35-40°C, stays firm at room temperature, and is firmer and more brittle: about 1 g per 100-150 ml for a firm set.
- **Pectin** jams need sugar and acid (or a low-sugar pectin with calcium).
- **Slurries:** 1 tbsp cornstarch in 2 tbsp cold water thickens about 1-1½ cups of simmering liquid to a sauce; simmer 1 minute. Flour slurries need about twice as much and 3-5 minutes of simmering.
- **Beurre manié** (equal parts soft butter and flour kneaded together) thickens a sauce at the end without lumps.

## Fats and frying

| Fat | Approx. smoke point | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Refined avocado oil | 270°C / 520°F | High-heat searing |
| Refined peanut oil | 230°C / 450°F | Classic frying and wok oil |
| Canola, vegetable, sunflower (refined) | 200-230°C / 400-450°F | Neutral all-purpose |
| Extra-virgin olive oil | 190-210°C / 375-410°F | Fine for sautéing, roasting and shallow frying; best flavor raw |
| Butter | ~150°C / 300°F | Milk solids burn; combine with oil or use clarified |
| Ghee / clarified butter | 250°C / 480°F | Nutty, high heat |
| Unrefined (toasted) sesame oil | low | Finishing only |
| Lard, beef tallow, duck fat | 190-205°C / 375-400°F | Savory; excellent for frying and potatoes |

- **Deep frying** at 175-190°C (350-375°F). Food drops the oil temperature, so fry in small batches and let it recover between them. A wooden chopstick bubbling steadily means ~175°C. Too cool gives greasy food; too hot burns the outside before the inside cooks. Drain on a rack, not paper (paper steams the bottom). Salt immediately.
- **Shallow frying** uses 0.5-1 cm of oil in a heavy skillet; same temperature logic.
- **Oil absorbed:** battered and breaded foods absorb roughly 10-15% of their weight in oil; fries about 5-8%. Use `@` in the nutrition engine (e.g., 1000 g oil @6% for fries cooked from 500 g potatoes).
- **Oven-frying and air-frying:** toss with a thin, even coat of oil (about 1 tsp per 100 g), cook in a single layer at 200-220°C, and flip once. You get most of the crunch with a fraction of the fat.
- **Fire:** smother a grease fire with a lid or baking soda, turn off the heat, and never use water.

## Enzymes

- **Browning enzymes** (polyphenol oxidase) brown cut apples, potatoes, avocado and artichokes. Stop it with acid (lemon), cold water, or contact exclusion (plastic wrap pressed onto guacamole).
- **Proteases** (pineapple bromelain, papaya papain, kiwi actinidin, fresh ginger, fig): tenderize in minutes, turn meat mushy in hours, and stop gelatin from setting.
- **Myrosinase** (mustard family: broccoli, cabbage, kale, radish, mustard, horseradish) creates pungency and the compound sulforaphane when the vegetable is cut. Heat destroys it: chop broccoli 30-40 minutes before cooking, or sprinkle a little mustard powder on cooked brassicas.
- **Alliinase** (garlic, onion) creates allicin when cut or crushed. Crushed garlic gets stronger as it sits; rest it 10 minutes before cooking for the most allicin.
- **Amylase** in egg yolk thins starch-thickened custards unless they're boiled.

## Fermentation and pickling

- **Lacto-fermented vegetables:** 2-3% salt by total weight (vegetables plus any water), submerged, 18-24°C, for days to weeks. Sauerkraut works well at 2-2.5% salt. Bubbles, sourness and cloudy brine are normal; fuzzy or colored mold, slime or a rotten smell are not.
- **Quick pickles** (refrigerator): equal parts 5% vinegar and water, 2-3% salt, sugar to taste, poured hot over sliced vegetables. Ready in 30 minutes (onions) to a day; keeps for weeks in the fridge. These are **not** shelf-stable; home canning requires tested recipes, and low-acid foods require pressure canning.
- **Yeast doughs:** ideal dough temperature is 24-26°C (75-78°F). A long cold rise in the fridge (12-72 hours) builds much more flavor. Sourdough acidity tightens gluten and adds flavor.

## Water, reduction and alcohol

- **Reduction concentrates everything that doesn't evaporate**, salt included: reducing by half doubles salinity. Salt reductions at the end. Long reduction drives off delicate aromatics, so add fresh ones back at the end.
- **Yields:** meat loses about 25-35% of its weight in cooking (more when well done or braised); onions lose 60-75% when caramelized; mushrooms about half; spinach collapses to roughly a tenth of its raw volume. See `numbers.md` for more.
- **Alcohol retention** (USDA): stirred into boiling liquid and removed from heat, ~85% remains; flambéed ~75%; baked or simmered 15 minutes ~40%, 30 minutes ~35%, 1 hour ~25%, 2 hours ~10%, 2.5 hours ~5%. Cooking never removes all of it; tell people who avoid alcohol and offer a swap (`access.md`).

## Food safety

- **Danger zone:** 4-60°C (40-140°F). Don't leave perishable food in it more than 2 hours (1 hour above 32°C / 90°F). Cool hot food fast: shallow containers, uncovered until steam stops, into the fridge within 2 hours.
- **Safe minimum internal temperatures** (USDA): poultry and stuffing 74°C (165°F); ground beef, pork and lamb 71°C (160°F); ground poultry 74°C; whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb and veal 63°C (145°F) with a 3-minute rest; fish 63°C (145°F) or opaque and flaking; egg dishes 71°C (160°F); reheated leftovers 74°C (165°F). Time-temperature equivalents are valid (see Proteins); rare whole-muscle beef is a lower risk than rare ground beef because surface bacteria aren't mixed into the center.
- **Fridge life:** cooked leftovers 3-4 days; raw poultry and ground meat 1-2 days; raw steaks, chops and roasts 3-5 days; raw fish 1-2 days; cooked rice up to 4 days, cooled quickly (Bacillus cereus spores survive cooking). Thaw in the fridge, in cold water changed every 30 minutes, or in the microwave, never on the counter.
- **Botulism:** garlic, herbs or chili in oil must be refrigerated and used within 4 days (or acidified). Don't improvise home canning.
- **Raw flour and raw eggs:** flour can carry E. coli. For edible raw cookie dough, heat-treat flour to 74°C (165°F). Use pasteurized eggs for raw-egg preparations (mayo, tiramisu, Caesar) when serving at-risk eaters.
- **Higher-risk eaters** (pregnancy, infants, elderly, immunocompromised): avoid raw or undercooked meat, fish, eggs and sprouts; unpasteurized dairy and juices; deli meats and hot dogs unless heated until steaming (Listeria); high-mercury fish (shark, swordfish, king mackerel, tilefish, bigeye tuna, marlin, orange roughy). Honey is unsafe for infants under 1 year.
- **Cross-contamination:** separate boards and plates for raw meat; wash hands, boards and knives in hot soapy water. Don't rinse raw chicken; it spreads bacteria.
- **Major allergens** (US): milk, egg, fish, crustacean shellfish, tree nuts, peanuts, wheat, soy, sesame. Mollusks, mustard, celery and lupin are major allergens in other regions (EU).

## Altitude

Above about 900 m (3,000 ft): water boils lower, so simmered and boiled foods take longer; batters rise faster and can collapse. For baking, reduce leavening by about 15-25%, reduce sugar slightly, add a little liquid, and raise oven temperature by 8-14°C (15-25°F) while shortening the bake. Candy and sugar temperatures drop about 1°C per 300 m (use the cold-water test).
