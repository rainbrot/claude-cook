# Flavor architecture

How deliciousness works and how to build it on purpose. Sections: Why we like what we like · Taste interactions · Umami · Heat and pungency · Aroma · Texture · Temperature · Balance · Cuisine grammars · Pairing and fusion · Familiar vs. novel · Meals and satiety.

## Why we like what we like

Taste is a nutrient detector, and pleasure tracks what was scarce and valuable:
- **Sweet** = sugars, fast energy. **Umami** = free glutamate and nucleotides, a signal of protein and of cooked, aged or fermented food. **Salty** = sodium, which the body cannot store. **Fat** (texture plus free-fatty-acid detection) = dense energy.
- **Sour** says ripe, fermented or spoiled. Mild sourness reads as fresh; strong sourness is liked only in balance.
- **Bitter** is a toxin alarm. It is liked in small, learned doses (coffee, greens, beer, char, dark chocolate) and works as contrast.

**Fat plus sugar is a combination nature almost never offers.** Fruit is sugar without fat; nuts, seeds and meat are fat with little sugar (milk is the main exception). In a 2018 study (DiFeliceantonio et al., *Cell Metabolism*), people valued fat-plus-carbohydrate foods more than calorie-matched foods with only fat or only carbohydrate, and reward circuits responded supra-additively. Fazzino et al. (2019) found three combinations that define most "hyper-palatable" food:
- fat + sodium: >25% of calories from fat and ≥0.30% sodium by weight (fries, cheese, cured meat, most well-seasoned savory dishes)
- fat + simple sugars: >20% of calories from fat and >20% from sugar (pastry, ice cream, salted caramel)
- carbohydrate + sodium: >40% of calories from carbohydrate and ≥0.20% sodium (pretzels, crackers, bread)

The engine flags these. How to use the idea:
- **Chef mode:** these combinations are why butter on bread, salted caramel, glazed pork belly and a crisp potato with aioli are irresistible. Deploy one on purpose as the hook. They are not a crutch for weak cooking.
- **Nutritionist mode:** lean on the levers that cost no calories, because they deliver pleasure without energy density. **Caloric levers:** fat, sugar, starch. **Near-zero-calorie levers:** salt (to target, not beyond), acid, umami (MSG, soy, miso, fish sauce, parmesan in small amounts), aroma (herbs, spices, alliums, zest, toasting), browning (Maillard flavor, not calories), heat (chili), bitterness, crunch made by roasting or air, temperature contrast, and visual appeal. Lean food becomes crave-worthy when those levers are turned all the way up.

## Taste interactions

| Add | What it does | Use it to |
|---|---|---|
| Salt | Raises aroma and sweetness perception, **suppresses bitterness** strongly | Fix flatness; tame bitter greens, eggplant, coffee and grapefruit with a pinch |
| Acid | Brightens and separates flavors, cuts fat, makes food taste lighter, slightly raises perceived saltiness | Finish braises, soups, rich sauces, fried food; balance sweetness |
| Sugar | Rounds harsh edges, suppresses sourness and bitterness, softens chili heat | Balance acidic tomatoes, vinegar, bitter or very spicy dishes; a few grams in savory food |
| Bitter | Adds adult complexity and cuts richness | Radicchio, charred edges, cocoa, coffee, beer, citrus pith against fat and sweetness |
| Fat | Carries fat-soluble aromas, coats the palate, mutes sharpness | Round out acid and heat; carry spice flavor; it needs a counterweight |
| Umami | Adds savory depth, persistence and "meatiness"; lets you use less salt | Deepen soups, sauces, vegetables, lean proteins |

Acids differ: citric (lemon, lime) is bright and clean; acetic (vinegar) is sharp and volatile, mellowing with heat; lactic (yogurt, buttermilk, sour cream, ferments) is round and creamy; malic (apple, rhubarb) is fruity; tartaric (wine, grapes, tamarind) is puckery. Hidden acid sources: tomato, wine, mustard, pickles and brine, kimchi, sumac, amchur, pomegranate molasses, capers, fermented chili sauces.

## Umami

Glutamate (savory base) multiplies with nucleotides (inosinate in meat and fish, guanylate in dried mushrooms). Mixed together they taste several times stronger than either alone.

- **Glutamate-rich:** kombu, parmesan and other aged cheeses, ripe and concentrated tomato (paste, sun-dried, roasted), soy sauce, miso, fish sauce, anchovy, nutritional yeast, MSG, fermented chili pastes, mushrooms, peas, corn, kimchi, aged cured meats, Worcestershire.
- **Inosinate:** chicken, pork, beef, bonito (katsuobushi), anchovy, sardine, tuna, shrimp and other seafood.
- **Guanylate:** dried shiitake (rehydrate in cold water for the most), dried porcini.

Proven synergies: dashi (kombu + bonito), tomato + parmesan + anchovy (puttanesca, Caesar), chicken stock + soy, burger + cheese + ketchup + mushrooms, Thai curry (fish sauce + shrimp paste + tomato), ramen (pork bones + kombu + shiitake), XO sauce. To build depth fast: 1 anchovy fillet melted into the oil, 1 tsp fish sauce or soy in a stew, a parmesan rind in soup, ½ tsp MSG per 4 servings, 1 tbsp tomato paste fried until brick-red.

Kokumi is "mouthfulness" (body and lingering continuity) from γ-glutamyl peptides in aged cheese, long-cooked broths, garlic, scallops and fermented foods. It is why a stock made with gelatin-rich parts tastes "fuller" at the same salt.

## Heat and pungency

- **Capsaicin** (chili) is fat- and alcohol-soluble, not water-soluble. It builds across bites (bite 1 mild means bite 10 hot), and warmth intensifies it. Casein (dairy), sugar, starch and fat soothe it; acid makes it brighter rather than milder. Heat is mostly in the pith and seeds: remove them to lower heat, not flavor.
- Chili characters differ: bright and green (jalapeño, serrano, Thai), fruity (habanero, Fresno), smoky (chipotle, smoked paprika), deep and fermented (gochujang, doubanjiang, sambal), raisiny and mild (ancho, guajillo), numbing (Sichuan peppercorn's sanshool is a tingle, not heat). Ask which heat the dish wants.
- **Isothiocyanates** (mustard, wasabi, horseradish, radish) are volatile and form when cut cells meet water. They fade with heat and time. Mustard powder needs 10 minutes in cold water to develop; hot liquid kills it.
- **Black pepper** (piperine) is best freshly ground and added late for aroma. **Ginger**: fresh is sharp (gingerol), cooked is mellow (zingerone), dried is hotter (shogaol).

## Aroma

Most of what people call flavor is smell through the back of the nose. Volatility decides when to add things.

- **Layering by timing** (think perfume):
  - *Base notes* (cooked low and long): sweated or browned alliums, bloomed whole spices, browned meat, tomato paste, aromatics in a braise.
  - *Heart notes* (developed by cooking): roasting, Maillard crust, reductions, toasted nuts and spices.
  - *Top notes* (added last, raw or barely warmed): tender herbs, citrus zest and juice, raw garlic or scallion, fresh chili, toasted sesame oil, finishing olive oil, a splash of vinegar, fish sauce, flaky salt.
- **Bloom spices in fat.** Most spice aroma compounds are fat-soluble. Toast whole spices dry until fragrant (30-90 seconds), or sizzle them in oil (tadka, chili oil). Ground spices burn in 30-60 seconds in hot oil, so add them with the onions or a splash of liquid.
- **Herbs:** hardy herbs (rosemary, thyme, sage, bay, oregano) cook in; tender herbs (basil, cilantro, parsley, mint, dill, chives, tarragon) go in at the end or raw. Use parsley and cilantro stems: they have more flavor than leaves and survive cooking.
- **Alliums:** raw is sharp and sulfurous; sweated is sweet; browned is savory-sweet; burnt is bitter. Garlic burns long before onions, so add it after the onions soften. Rinse or soak sliced raw onion in cold water or vinegar for 10 minutes to remove harshness.
- **Citrus:** zest is aroma (oils, no acid) and juice is acid (little aroma). Use both for full citrus character. Add juice off the heat.
- **Smoke and char** add aroma cheaply: charred peppers, scallions and lemons, blistered tomatoes, smoked paprika, chipotle.

## Texture

Contrast is one of the strongest predictors of how much people like a dish. "Dynamic contrast" (food that changes in the mouth: shatters then melts, crunches then turns creamy, bursts) is especially pleasurable, which is why crisp-edged things, ice cream and fried chicken are so loved.

- Every plate wants at least one crisp or crunchy element and one soft, creamy or saucy element.
- Cheap crunch: toasted nuts or seeds; breadcrumbs or panko toasted in butter or oil (pangrattato); fried shallots or garlic; crispy chickpeas; frico (crisped cheese); croutons; crushed tortilla chips, crackers or potato chips; raw crunchy vegetables (cucumber, radish, cabbage, celery, fennel, snap peas); crispy skin.
- Crispness is a water problem: dry the surface, use high heat, leave space, cool on a rack, add sauce at the last moment, and never cover fried food while it is hot.
- Body and viscosity: gelatin (stock from wings or bones), starch (pasta water, a cornstarch slurry), emulsified fat, puréed vegetables, egg yolk, reduction.
- Softness that is not mush: cook vegetables to tender-crisp, braise to just fork-tender, rest proteins.

## Temperature

- Warmth increases perceived sweetness and aroma; cold mutes both. Season cold dishes harder (potato salad, gazpacho, ice-cream bases taste too sweet when warm and right when frozen).
- Hot-cold contrast is a hook: à la mode, cold yogurt on hot curry, warm chocolate sauce on ice cream.
- Many things taste best warm or at room temperature rather than hot: roasted vegetables, grain salads, cheese, tomatoes (never refrigerate ripe tomatoes unless they are about to spoil).

## Balance

Think in two axes: **rich** (fat, sugar, starch, umami) against **bright** (acid, bitterness, pungency, fresh herbs, crunch).
- Every heavy dish needs a cutting element. Every lean or sharp dish needs something round.
- Match intensity: delicate proteins (white fish, scallops, eggs) with gentle flavors; bold ones (lamb, beef, oily fish) with bold flavors.
- **Echo with variation** for depth and coherence: one ingredient in two forms (lemon zest plus preserved lemon, roasted plus raw fennel, cooked plus fresh chili, browned butter plus a final knob of cold butter).
- **Restraint:** three to five strong flavors in a dish. Past that it gets muddy unless a cuisine's grammar holds it together (curry, mole).
- A dish tastes flat? Diagnose in order: salt, acid, umami, aroma (fresh herbs, zest, a sizzle of spiced oil), texture (crunch). See `rescue.md`.

## Pairing and fusion

- **What grows together goes together**: same season, same region (tomato and basil, apple and pork, lamb and rosemary, corn and chili and lime).
- **Shared aroma compounds** are a weak heuristic. Western recipes tend to combine ingredients that share compounds; East Asian recipes tend to avoid it (Ahn et al., 2011). Build pairings on roles and contrast first.
- **Fusion that works** keeps one cuisine's structure (the dish format and technique) and borrows another's seasoning through a **bridge ingredient** that belongs to both: lime and cilantro (Mexican and Thai/Vietnamese), sesame (Korean, Japanese, Levantine tahini), cumin (Indian, Mexican, North African), yogurt (Indian, Levantine, Greek), chili crisp or gochujang standing in for chipotle, miso standing in for parmesan (both fermented glutamate bombs), coconut (South Indian, Thai, Caribbean).
- Reliable, proven combinations: chocolate with chili, orange, coffee or salt; strawberry with balsamic or black pepper; watermelon, feta and mint; pear and blue cheese; beet, goat cheese and walnut; carrot and cumin; fennel and orange; peas and mint; salmon and dill; duck and cherry; peanut, lime and chili; mushroom and thyme; corn, lime and cotija.

## Familiar and novel

- **Most advanced yet acceptable**: anchor one surprise inside a familiar frame. A dish should be recognizable from its name; the surprise is in one component (a gochujang butter on corn, brown butter in a vinaigrette, miso in cookies).
- Expectation changes taste. Color, plating, a confident name and aroma at the table (a final grating of zest, a hot spoon of spiced oil) raise perceived flavor.
- Nostalgia is a powerful lever for comfort food: keep the core of the remembered version intact.

## Meals and satiety

- **Sensory-specific satiety**: pleasure from one flavor fades as you eat it, while other flavors stay appealing. Keep each bite interesting with small, intense counterpoints (pickles, herbs, chili crisp, a squeeze of lime), and give a multi-dish meal a range of textures, temperatures and tastes.
- **Arc of a meal**: lighter and brighter first, richest in the middle, refreshing or sweet to finish. Don't repeat the main flavor or texture across courses.
- **Portion of the hook**: small amounts of something intense (a spoon of salsa macha, a few fried capers) do more than large amounts of something mild.
